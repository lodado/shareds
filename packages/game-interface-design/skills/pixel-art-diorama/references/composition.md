# Composition

Where things go on the screen so the eye lands where the sheet says. No code. Every number here is a
starting value from general pixel-art and illustration practice, not measured in this skill; change one
only with a reason written on the direction sheet.

## Value plan

Decide the light and dark before any colour. Squint at the wide shot: it should read as three to five
flat grey masses.

- The stage gets the widest value range in the frame (a near-black and a near-white both present).
  Everything around it is compressed into the middle: no darker than the stage's darks, no lighter than
  its lights.
- Two neighbouring masses never share a value unless they are one object. A grey wall in front of a
  grey road is one lump at half size.
- The landmark is either the lightest or the darkest large mass, never a middle one, so it separates
  from the sky behind it.
- Sky and ground differ in value by at least two ramp steps; hue alone does not hold at 270 px.
- The accent is the only saturated colour in the stage zone. Its value sits outside the stage's usual
  range, so it still reads in grayscale.

## Frame and corners

A 45° camera turns the world into a diamond, and the diamond leaves an empty triangle in each corner of
a wide frame. Decide per mode, on the sheet:

- Whole-world mode: keep the whole diamond and spend the corners on something that belongs to the
  world (water, sky, clouds, far ground), or crop the diamond with a tighter half-height so no corner is
  empty. Do not leave flat background colour.
- Follow and close modes: the subject sits a little below the centre of the frame, with more room above
  it than below, because buildings rise upward from the ground plane.
- Leave a margin of at least 6% of the frame height between the world's edge and the frame edge in the
  whole-world mode, so the island or the quay does not look cut off by accident.
- The stage occupies roughly a third to a half of the frame width in the follow mode. Smaller loses the
  action; larger leaves no setting.

## Silhouette and rhythm

- The landmark must be recognisable filled with one flat colour. If it is not, change its outline: a
  tower, a notch, a pitched roof, a chimney, never a plain box.
- Masses along an edge alternate tall, low, tall. Three equal heights in a row read as a fence.
- One large shape, two or three medium, many small. Sizes that are all the same read as noise.
- Break straight rooflines with one element per building (a chimney, a sign, a vent). One, not five.
- A horizon line that runs across the frame at exactly half height splits the picture. Put the main
  edge at about a third or two thirds.

## Depth without perspective

The camera is orthographic, so size does not shrink with distance. Depth comes from:

- Overlap: place something near in front of something far, in every rotation the sheet lists.
- Value and saturation: far masses lose contrast and saturation by about a ramp step; near ones keep
  full contrast.
- Shadow: one cast shadow under each large mass anchors it to the ground and separates it from what is
  behind.
- Detail: trim level only toward the stage and the camera; far faces keep mass and part levels
  ([world dressing](world-dressing.md)).

## Hero rotation

The four rotations cannot all be the best picture.

- Name one rotation the hero: the landmark behind the stage, the main light raking across it. Polish it
  first and let screenshots and demos open in it.
- The other three must pass a lower bar, written on the sheet: the stage is whole, the landmark still
  names the place, no mass hides the subject, and nothing hangs in the air where a wall dropped.
- If the sun sits on the hero side, the opposite rotations show shadow faces: check that their darks do
  not fall below the palette's darkest ramp step and merge with the outline.

## Eye path

- Lines point at the focal point: court lines, a quay edge, a path, a row of lamps. Remove or reroute a
  line that points at nothing.
- Warm, bright, saturated and moving things pull the eye first. Give that set to what the sheet ranks
  first and keep the rest of the frame out of it.
- A vertical element (a flagpole, a mast) beside the focal point frames it; one on the far side of the
  frame pulls the eye away.
- Rest areas: at least two quiet zones, empty or one-colour, where the eye can leave the busy area.
  Write them on the sheet ([world dressing](world-dressing.md)).

## Page layout

The page around the canvas follows the same value plan. The rules for the controls are in
[art direction](art-direction.md); this is the layout.

- Editorial (wide screens): a thin mast with the name and one line, the canvas as the hero at 60–70% of
  the content width, and a narrow control rail beside it or under it, on a grid of 8 px steps. The page
  background is the lightest neutral of the palette and the text is its darkest; the canvas gets a
  one-step darker frame so it reads as a picture, not a hole.
- One type scale, three sizes at most (name, label, body) in a ratio of about 1.25 to 1.5, a generous
  line height for body text, and no more than two weights.
- Phone: the canvas first at full width, then the controls in a single column wrapped in groups with the
  group name above each, then the feed. The canvas never scrolls sideways and keeps its whole-pixel
  enlargement.
- The accent colour appears once on the page chrome: the one primary action. Segmented controls use
  neutral fills and a darker fill for the selected option, with the selection shown by more than colour
  alone.
